I have ruined plenty of decent emails with lazy subject lines.

Not because the email body was bad. Usually the opposite. I would spend 20 minutes writing a careful message, tweaking the ask, trimming the paragraphs, making sure it sounded human — and then I would type something like "Quick question" in five seconds and call it done.

That is such a bad trade.

The subject line is the first decision point. Before someone reads your opener, before they notice your CTA, before they appreciate how thoughtful your email is, they make a snap call about whether this message deserves attention at all.

When I look back at the subject lines that consistently work better, they are rarely "clever." They usually just do three jobs well.

## The 3 things every great subject line does

### 1. It creates urgency from context, not from hype

A lot of bad subject lines try to manufacture urgency with volume: caps, exclamation marks, pressure words, fake scarcity.

That is not the kind of urgency people trust.

The versions that work better usually point to something real:

- "Your trial expires Friday — here is what changes"
- "Before your board meeting Thursday"
- "Quick thought before your launch next week"

Those feel urgent because there is an actual reason to pay attention now. "URGENT!!!" feels urgent because the sender is yelling.

### 2. It is specific enough to feel like it belongs to one person

Generic subject lines used to get away with more. Not anymore.

"Quick question," "Checking in," and "Thought you would find this interesting" all blur together now. They do not give the recipient a reason to believe the email is about *them*.

Specificity is what changes that.

Compare these:

- Bad: "Quick question"
- Better: "Question about your Q3 hiring plan"

- Bad: "Thought you would find this interesting"
- Better: "Your competitor just launched a free tier — one thing stood out"

I like one simple test here: could you send this exact subject line to 100 people without changing anything? If yes, it is probably too generic.

### 3. It survives the phone screen test

A lot of emails are first seen on mobile, which means your subject line is fighting for maybe 30-40 visible characters.

That is why front-loading matters so much.

- Bad: "I wanted to reach out because your team is hiring..."
- Better: "Your dev team is hiring — one candidate idea"

- Bad: "Following up on our conversation from the conference..."
- Better: "SaaStr follow-up: the pricing tool I mentioned"

If the valuable part of the subject only shows up after character 45, a lot of people will never see it.

## Subject line formulas I keep coming back to

I do not believe in one magic template, but there are a few patterns I reach for repeatedly because they sound natural and give the recipient a reason to open.

### [Their thing] + question

Example: **"Your podcast episode on pricing — quick follow-up"**

This works when there is a genuine trigger. It shows you are replying to something specific, not spraying a template.

### Specific number + benefit

Example: **"Cut your support tickets by 40%?"**

This can work well when the number is real and the benefit is understandable in one glance. If the number feels made up, it backfires fast.

### Mutual context

Example: **"Sarah suggested I reach out"**

Warm context still beats cold context. The trick is to use it naturally, not like borrowed credibility.

### Observation + curiosity

Example: **"Noticed your pricing page skips enterprise"**

I like this one because it feels grounded. You saw something. You have a reason for the email. That is enough.

### Time-anchored

Example: **"Before your Series B announcement"**

This works best when the timing is real. If the deadline is fake, the whole email starts to smell fake too.

## Subject lines I have mostly stopped using

There are a few subject lines I almost never use now, even though they used to feel harmless.

**"Quick question"** — too generic, too overused, and too closely associated with automation.

**"Following up"** — fine inside a real ongoing thread, weak as a standalone subject because it gives the recipient nothing new to care about.

**"Hope you are doing well"** — better as a sentence in the body than as the headline for the entire email.

**Fake `RE:` or `FW:`** — not worth it. Even if a filter misses it, the recipient notices.

**`[Their company] x [Your company]`** — unless the relationship is obvious already, it usually reads like partnership-template filler.

## How I test subject lines before sending

I do not have a dramatic process for this. I just try not to trust my first draft too much.

**Real-time scoring.** If I am writing in Gmail, I want feedback while the email is still open in front of me. That is why EmailSubjectScore Pro shows spam risk, clarity, and urgency beneath the subject field as I type.

![EmailSubjectScore Pro showing spam, clarity, and urgency scores below a Gmail subject line](/assets/blog/emailsubjectscore-cws.png)

**A/B testing in small batches.** If two subject lines seem equally good, I split the send and see which one gets better replies. Not forever. Just enough to stop pretending I can always guess right.

**The phone test.** I look at the subject the way a recipient probably will: as a tiny line on a crowded phone screen. If the important words are buried, I rewrite.

**Ask one other human.** This sounds almost too simple, but it works. Show someone two or three options and ask which one they would open first.

## Common mistakes by email type

A few patterns show up over and over.

### Cold outreach
People try to be mysterious. Usually it is better to be clear.

### Follow-ups
People reuse "Following up" when they should be adding a new reason to care.

### Networking emails
People force the connection too hard instead of sounding like a normal person.

### Internal emails
People go vague on the emails that actually need clarity most. If there is a deadline or decision, put it in the subject.

## The 60-second subject line audit

![Subject line formulas and examples for cold email outreach](/assets/blog/best-email-subject-line-extensions-hero.png)

Before I send an important email, I try to give the subject line one quick pass:

1. **Character count** — does the important part show up early?
2. **Spam phrases** — anything that sounds like automation?
3. **Specificity** — could this only belong to this person or company?
4. **Urgency** — is there a real reason to open now?
5. **Gut check** — if a stranger sent this to me, would I open it?

That one minute is usually worth more than another ten minutes polishing the body.
