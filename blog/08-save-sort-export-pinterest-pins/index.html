How to Save, Sort, and Export Pinterest Pins for Later

2026-11-16
#pinterest#pinterest-pins#pinterest-organization#pinterest-research#pinello

Saving a Pinterest Pin takes a second. Finding the useful one again can take much longer.

That is why a good save workflow needs more than a growing collection of boards. You need a reason for saving, a way to narrow the list later, and an export path when your own research needs to leave the browser.

Pinterest already provides the basic save and board tools. Pinello adds a local Favorites workflow for creators who want to keep scored and unscored examples nearby while they browse. The two systems serve different jobs: Pinterest is the place where Pins and boards live; Pinello is a private working list for inspection and pre-publish research.

Pinello is a free Chrome extension that scores Pinterest pins across 5 dimensions — image, board fit, title, description, and hashtags — in real time.

1. Save a Pin to a Pinterest board

Pinterest’s official Help Center describes the normal desktop flow: hover over a Pin or open it in closeup, select the save control, and choose a board. You can also create a new board from the save flow. On mobile, open the Pin, tap Save, and select an existing or new board.

Start by choosing the board that best describes the Pin’s subject, not merely the board you opened most recently. If you are collecting examples for a product launch, “small bedroom storage” is more useful than a broad “ideas” board. If you are saving recipe references, a board organized around the meal or audience may be easier to revisit than one named only “food.”

Boards are public or private depending on your settings and purpose. A private board can be useful for early research, drafts, or ideas you are not ready to share. A collaborative board has a different purpose: it lets invited people contribute. Decide which kind of collection you are building before you fill it.

Saving a Pin to Pinterest does not download the image to your computer. It keeps the Pin in a board so you can find it on Pinterest later.

2. Give every save a reason

The fastest way to make a board noisy is to save everything that looks vaguely relevant.

Before saving, finish this sentence:

I am saving this because…

Useful answers include:

  • the image makes the product understandable at thumbnail size
  • the title names a specific audience or problem
  • the board is tightly focused
  • the description adds useful context
  • the Pin is a good reference for a format you want to test
  • the destination page matches the promise of the Pin

This habit separates inspiration from research. A mood board can be broad and emotional. A working library should help you make a decision later.

Also save a few imperfect examples. A Pin with a strong image and a vague title can teach you where the draft loses clarity. A Pin with an interesting topic but a weak board match can show you what to check before you publish. If you save only polished references, your collection may look good while giving you fewer practical comparisons.

3. Sort your Pinterest boards

Pinterest’s official board-organization guide explains that boards can be sorted automatically by options such as A to Z, Custom, or Last saved to. On desktop, you can also drag boards into a custom order. Mobile offers comparable sorting and drag-and-drop controls.

Use board order to reflect your current work, not an imaginary permanent taxonomy. Put active projects or current seasonal topics where you can reach them quickly. Move archive boards lower. If you have boards for clients or shops, group them in a way that reduces accidental saves to the wrong place.

Board order is not the same as Pin order. A tidy profile does not automatically make every board useful. Review the board name, description, sections, and recent saves as separate pieces of organization.

Pinterest may also show unorganized ideas that can be reviewed or moved. Treat that area as a queue, not a final library. A Pin that stays unclassified for months is harder to use when you need it.

4. Use Pinello Favorites for the working shortlist

Pinterest boards are good for keeping a Pin in its platform context. Pinello Favorites are useful when you want a smaller local shortlist while you inspect examples and prepare your own draft.

From the feed or Pin detail view, you can save a Pin to Favorites. Pinello can keep the Pin URL, thumbnail, title, board information, saved date, and available score context. A Pin can be useful even when Pinterest does not expose enough data for a complete score, so unscored references should not be discarded automatically.

Sort by score when you need to review the strongest matches to Pinello’s checks first. Sort by title when you are looking for a topic. Filter the list to reduce a large collection to the few Pins relevant to the draft in front of you.

Do not treat the score as a creative verdict. A low-scoring Pin may contain a useful composition or product idea. A high-scoring Pin may be irrelevant to your audience. Use the score to choose what to inspect next, then read the Pin yourself.

Pinello Favorites are local to the browser profile. They are not a Pinterest backup service, and they are not a shared team database. If you clear local extension data or uninstall the extension, local records may no longer be available. Export your own data when you need a separate copy.

5. Know the difference between save, download, and export

These three actions are often confused:

Save keeps a Pin on a Pinterest board so you can find it on Pinterest.

Download saves an available image file to your device. Pinterest’s official download guide says to use the ellipsis menu and choose Download image. Not every Pin has the option, and Pinterest currently does not offer a separate original-resolution download for every image. Video Pins have different limitations.

Export creates a structured copy of information for use elsewhere, such as a CSV for your own spreadsheet.

Downloading an image is not the same as exporting a board. Pinterest’s official help pages document saving and individual image downloads, but they do not promise a universal one-click export of every board and every metadata field. Be cautious with third-party downloaders: check permissions, privacy policies, licensing, and the current Pinterest rules before using one.

6. Export your own Pinello research

If you use Pinello Pro, CSV export can move scored Pins and Favorites into a spreadsheet. That is useful for a personal research log, a content planning sheet, or a list of references to review with a collaborator.

Think of the CSV as your local record, not as Pinterest Analytics. It may include the information Pinello could verify or store at the time you saved the Pin. It does not add impressions, outbound clicks, conversions, or account-level performance that Pinterest has not provided.

A simple spreadsheet structure can include:

Column Why keep it
Pin URL Return to the original Pin
Saved date Remember when you collected it
Topic Group the research question
Board Preserve the visible context
Pinello score Narrow a review list
Observation Record what you learned
Test idea Turn research into your own next step

Add your own notes after exporting. “Clear product in first frame” is more useful than “high score.” “Board matches exact customer problem” is more useful than “popular.” Your notes should describe what you can verify, not what you assume caused the Pin’s response.

7. Make a weekly cleanup routine

A useful library does not need to be enormous.

Once a week, open your Pinterest boards or Pinello Favorites and remove duplicates, irrelevant references, and Pins you no longer understand. Then choose three examples and write one sentence for each:

  1. What is clear in the image?
  2. What is clear or unclear in the title and description?
  3. What would I test differently in my own Pin?

If you publish for several products, use separate board or spreadsheet areas for each audience. Mixing every topic into one research list makes the save count feel productive while making the actual review slower.

8. Use the library before publishing

When you are ready to create a Pin, search your saved examples for patterns rather than copying a design.

Look for repeated choices:

  • Are titles direct, descriptive, or question-led?
  • Do boards use a narrow topic or a broad category?
  • Is the product shown alone, in context, or as a step-by-step?
  • Do descriptions explain the destination?
  • Are topics and hashtags related to the same central idea?

Then open Create Pin and review your own draft. Pinello’s Pin Doctor checks the image, board fit, title, description, and hashtags or topics. If you use an AI action, it is user-triggered and copy-only. You review the wording and choose whether to paste it; Pinello does not fill fields or publish automatically.

The purpose is not to recreate another creator’s Pin. It is to make your own draft easier to understand and easier for you to review.

A practical setup

Use Pinterest boards for durable platform organization. Use Pinello Favorites for a local working shortlist. Use a CSV export when you need your own records in a spreadsheet. Use Pinterest Analytics after publishing when you need observed account or Pin results.

That division keeps each tool honest. A saved Pin is a reference. A downloaded image is a local file whose rights and context still matter. A CSV is your research record. Analytics is measurement. None of these is a guarantee of reach or ranking.

For a browser-side shortlist that stays close to your Pin workflow, try Pinello. Save references, narrow them with local sorting and filtering, and review your next draft before publishing.
